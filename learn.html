Market order vs. limit order
Market order
Seeks a prompt fill at available prices. The execution price can differ from the price you saw.
Limit order
Sets the most you will pay on a buy or the least you will accept on a sell. It may not fill.

Example: If the lowest available offer moves from $50 to $50.20, a market buy may fill above $50; a $50 limit buy cannot pay more than $50.

ETF vs. mutual fund pricing
Exchange-traded fund (ETF)
Retail investors trade shares on an exchange during its session. The market price can be above or below net asset value (NAV) per share.
Mutual fund
Purchases and redemptions use the fund’s next calculated NAV, typically determined once per business day, before any applicable fees or charges.

Example: An ETF can trade at $100.20 when its most recently published NAV is $100. A mutual fund order does not lock in that earlier $100 NAV; it uses the next one.

Bond coupon rate vs. current yield
Coupon rate
For a fixed-rate bond, annual coupon payments divided by face value.
Current yield
Annual coupon payments divided by today’s market price. It does not count any price gain or loss at maturity.

Example: A $1,000 face-value bond paying $50 per year has a 5% coupon rate. At a $950 market price, its current yield is about 5.26%. Yield to maturity also takes the repayment and the time remaining into account.
